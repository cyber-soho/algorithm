Lesson 1: Dijkstra vs. Floyd-Warshall

This section compares the two foundational algorithms for finding the cheapest routes across a network (graph). You will explore their mechanics, limitations, and real-world Big-O scalability.

Dijkstra's Algorithm

O(E log V)

Built by Edsger W. Dijkstra (1956)

Strategy: Greedy. Always expands the closest unvisited node. Answers "One source → all destinations."

The Three Rules:
  1. Start with source distance = 0, all others = ∞
  2. Pick unvisited node with smallest tentative distance
  3. Relax neighbours, mark visited, repeat
Best For: Sparse, large graphs (Maps, GPS)
Limitation: Negative edge weights break it

Floyd-Warshall

O(V³)

Built by Robert Floyd & Stephen Warshall (1962)

Strategy: Dynamic Programming. Considers each node as a possible intermediate stopover. Answers "All sources → all destinations."

How it scales: O(V³)
  • 100 nodes = 1 million operations
  • 1,000 nodes = 1 billion operations
Best For: Dense, small graphs (Pre-computed tables)
Advantage: Negative weights allowed (no negative cycles)

Industry Anchor: When you search a route on Google Maps (e.g., Madrid Metro: Sol → Aeropuerto T4), variants of Dijkstra answer the call.

Lesson 2: Network Routing Protocols

The internet is a graph of routers. Protocols determine how computers "gossip" about geography to agree on paths. Interact with the selector below to compare the two main mathematical approaches, and learn how the public internet differs with BGP.

The Exception: BGP (Border Gateway Protocol)

Inside an organisation, math (like Dijkstra) chooses paths. Between organisations on the public internet, contracts do.

  • ■ BGP connects autonomous systems (ISPs, Cloud providers).
  • ■ It is Policy-Based, not strictly shortest-path. A physically longer path might be chosen for business reasons.
  • ■ Vulnerable: BGP misconfigurations frequently cause major internet outages.

"It is the protocol you blame when the internet is slow today."

This section explores the theoretical "wall" of computer science. Some problems are not just hard to code; they are hard by nature. Click the flashcards below to understand the core complexity classes that define modern algorithm engineering.

The Brutal Reality of Exponential Growth

Adding just ONE more input element doubles the runtime to 72 years.

Complexity Classes (Click to Reveal)

P

Polynomial

Solvable in reasonable time.

Examples: Sorting arrays, Dijkstra's Shortest Path.

NP

Nondeterministic Polynomial

Solution can be VERIFIED quickly.

Examples: Sudoku, Scheduling, TSP.

NP-complete

The Hardest of NP

Solve one fast, you solve them ALL fast.

Examples: TSP Decision, Boolean SAT. (Cook's Theorem 1971)

NP-hard

Beyond NP

At least as hard as NP-complete. May not even be verifiable.

Examples: Halting Problem, Optimisation TSP.

The $1 Million Question: P = NP?

Is every problem whose solution can be checked quickly also one that can be solved quickly? Unsolved since 1971. Most experts bet P ≠ NP. Recognizing these shapes (Knapsack, SAT, Scheduling) saves your career: it tells you to stop promising exact fast solutions and start promising good approximate ones.

The Engineering Trade-off: Three Lanes

1. Brute Force

  • Time Cost: Astronomical
  • Quality: Provably optimal
  • Method: Try every combination.
Use: Toy sizes (< 15), Ground-truth tests

2. Approximation

  • Time Cost: Polynomial (Fast enough)
  • Quality: Provably close (e.g. ≤ 1.5× optimum via Christofides)
  • Method: Mathematical bounds.
Use: Production where bounds matter

3. Heuristic

  • Time Cost: Very Fast O(n²)
  • Quality: Usually decent (15-25% worse), NO guarantee.
  • Method: "Nearest Neighbour", Genetic algos.
Use: Real-time, "good enough" wins

"Senior engineers are not the ones with bigger algorithms — they are the ones who know which lane to pick."
