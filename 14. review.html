Originating lectures: Module 08 / Week 10.

This chapter is about two questions that algorithms answer constantly: "is this thing in my data?" (searching) and "which option should I pick right now?" (greedy decision-making). The chapter is the first one in the exam syllabus where time-complexity classes start to matter in a way you can feel — the gap between O(n) and O(log n) is not abstract once you have actually waited for a search to finish on a list of a million items.

1.1 Sequential Search (Linear Search)

The plainest possible search idea. You walk through the data one item at a time, from index 0 to the end. You stop the moment you find the target, or you finish the list having never found it. Two practical truths follow:

  • The data does not need to be sorted. This is the algorithm's freedom — and the cost of it.
  • The worst case is checking every single item. This is the algorithm's tax.

Time complexity:

Case Big-O Meaning
Best O(1) ("Big-O of one", constant time) Target sits at index 0. One comparison and you are done.
Average O(n) ("Big-O of n", linear time) On a random input, you check about half the list.
Worst O(n) ("Big-O of n", linear time) Target is at the end, or absent. You check every item.

Sequential search is the right tool when (a) the list is small (say, fewer than ~30 items), (b) the list is so unsorted that pre-sorting it costs more than the searches you will do on it, or (c) you only ever search once. It is also, almost always, the first algorithm a CS student implements in their life, which is why it remains on every exam.

1.2 Binary Search

Binary search is the moment newcomers feel the power of an algorithm for the first time. The idea is simple: the data must be sorted, and you keep cutting the search space in half until you either land on the target or there is nothing left to search.

The algorithm in plain English:

  1. Take the middle element of the current range.
  2. If it equals the target — done, return its index.
  3. If the target is smaller — throw away the right half, repeat on the left half.
  4. If the target is larger — throw away the left half, repeat on the right half.
  5. If the range becomes empty, the target is not in the list.

Time complexity:

Case Big-O Meaning
Best O(1) Target is the middle element on the first try.
Average O(log n) ("Big-O of log n", logarithmic) Each step halves the search space.
Worst O(log n) Same logic — even the worst case is logarithmic.

The headline fact for the exam: doubling the input adds only one extra step. A list of 1 million → about 20 steps. A list of 1 billion → about 30 steps. Compare that with sequential search, which would need 1 million or 1 billion steps respectively.

Binary search is a real-world workhorse: built-in routines like bsearch() in C, Arrays.binarySearch() in Java, and bisect.bisect_left() in Python all implement variants of it. A famous engineering note from Google Research — "Nearly all binary searches and merge sorts are broken" by Joshua Bloch (2006) — showed that even professionals get the +1 / -1 boundary conditions wrong, so write the loop carefully.

1.4 The Greedy Algorithm Strategy

A greedy algorithm is one that, at every step, makes the choice that looks best right now — what we call the locally optimal choice — in the hope that the sum of those choices will be the globally optimal outcome. There is no backtracking, no peeking into the future, and once a choice is committed, the algorithm never reconsiders it.

For greedy to be provably optimal, the problem must have two properties:

  • Greedy choice property — making the locally optimal pick at each step is always a valid step toward the globally optimal solution.
  • Optimal substructure — the optimal solution to the whole contains within it the optimal solutions to its parts (we will see this property again in Chapter 2, where it is the gatekeeper for Dynamic Programming).

Classic greedy algorithms that do give optimal answers:

  • Coin change with canonical denominations (e.g., the standard US (United States) or EUR (Euro) coin sets): always take the largest coin that fits.
  • Activity Selection Problem: sort jobs by end time, then always pick the next job that starts no earlier than the previous one ended. Guaranteed optimal.
  • Dijkstra's algorithm: greedy single-source shortest path (covered in Chapter 4). Optimal when all edge weights are non-negative.
  • Kruskal's and Prim's algorithms for Minimum Spanning Tree (MST): always pick the cheapest edge that does not form a cycle.
  • Huffman coding for lossless data compression — used in ZIP, JPEG, and MP3 formats among many others.

Classic problems where greedy fails:

  • 0/1 Knapsack — picking by value-per-weight ratio sometimes leaves capacity unused, which a smarter (DP) solution would have used.
  • Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP) — "always go to the nearest unvisited city" is roughly 25% worse than optimal on average and arbitrarily bad in adversarial cases.
  • Coin change with non-canonical denominations — for coin set {1¢, 3¢, 4¢} and target 6¢, greedy picks {4, 1, 1} = 3 coins; the optimal is {3, 3} = 2 coins.

Typical time complexity of a greedy algorithm: O(n) if no pre-sort is needed, O(n log n) if a sort is required (which is the most common case). Space: usually O(1) to O(n).

Exam-ready summary: greedy is fast and easy to code but only correct on problems with the greedy-choice and optimal-substructure properties. When in doubt, ask "is there a counter-example?" — if you can construct one, do not use greedy.

Originating lecture: Module 07 / Week 09.

Dynamic Programming (DP) is the answer to a quietly common pain in computing: doing the same work over and over again because you keep forgetting that you already did it. The technique was named by Richard Bellman in the 1950s — the name is intentionally opaque, since "dynamic programming" had a friendlier political ring than "mathematical optimisation" at the time he was funded by the US (United States) Department of Defense.

2.1 The Two Properties That Make DP Work

A problem is a DP candidate if and only if it has both of the following:

  • Overlapping subproblems. The same smaller sub-questions appear repeatedly while solving the larger problem. Fibonacci is the canonical example: computing fib(10) re-computes fib(8), fib(7), etc. dozens of times in the naive recursion.
  • Optimal substructure. (Same definition as in Chapter 1.) The optimal answer to the whole problem can be built from the optimal answers to its parts.

Lose either property and DP is the wrong tool. The Longest Simple Path problem in a graph, for example, has overlapping subproblems but lacks optimal substructure — picking the longest A-to-B segment can block the B-to-C segment because no node can be revisited. That is exactly why finding longest simple paths is much harder than shortest paths.

2.2 Two Implementation Flavours

Both flavours eliminate repeated work; they differ in how they remember.

Memoization — top-down, recursive, with a cache.

  • You write the natural recursive solution.
  • Before computing anything, you check a cache (a dict, HashMap, or array).
  • If the answer is cached, return it directly.
  • Otherwise compute, store, and return.
  • Lazy evaluation: only the sub-problems actually asked for ever get solved.
  • Risk: very deep recursion can blow the call stack (stack overflow).

Tabulation — bottom-up, iterative, with a table.

  • You identify the order in which sub-problems can be solved without contradictions (smallest first).
  • You fill an array or matrix in that order, each cell built from already-computed cells.
  • Eager evaluation: every sub-problem is solved whether you "need" it or not.
  • Advantage: no recursion, often cache-friendlier, more memory-predictable.
Feature Memoization Tabulation
Direction Top-down Bottom-up
Mechanism Recursive function + cache Iterative loop + table
Evaluation Lazy (on demand) Eager (all sub-problems)
Stack-overflow risk Yes No
Memory pattern Hash maps / sparse arrays Dense arrays — cache-friendly
Easier to write? Usually yes Requires careful ordering

In an exam, if you are asked "which DP style?", look at two things: (a) does the problem only need a subset of sub-problems? — favours memoization. (b) Is recursion depth a concern? — favours tabulation.

A short mantra to memorise: "Greedy is for now; DP is for everything."

2.5 DP in the Real World — Networking

The most beautiful real-world DP story is the Bellman-Ford algorithm, named after Richard Bellman and Lester Ford. It is a direct DP solution to the single-source shortest-path problem that handles negative edge weights (Dijkstra cannot). Bellman-Ford is the algorithmic backbone of the RIP (Routing Information Protocol) routing protocol, standardised by the IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force), and a close cousin of EIGRP (Enhanced Interior Gateway Routing Protocol) developed by Cisco.

In a related industrial story, QoS (Quality of Service) routing extends shortest-path DP to handle multiple constraints simultaneously — minimise delay while guaranteeing a minimum bandwidth, for example. The IETF documented these requirements as RFC (Request for Comments) 2386.

Finally, server-job scheduling at cloud providers like AWS (Amazon Web Services) and Microsoft Azure is a variant of the Knapsack Problem — itself a textbook DP problem. Whenever a Kubernetes cluster scheduler decides which pod fits on which node, a DP-flavoured optimisation is humming away in the background.

This chapter holds the most interview-popular data structures in the field. If a recruiter asks one technical question, it will almost certainly involve a hash table or a graph.

3.1 Hash Functions and Collision Handling

A hash function is a deterministic recipe that maps any input — a string, a phone number, an entire file — to a fixed-size number called a hash value or hash code. The same input always produces the same hash. That single property is what makes a hash table possible.

A hash table is a long row of memory slots. To store value V under key K, you compute slot = hash(K) mod table_size and place V there. To look it up later, you re-compute the same slot and read it back. Average-case look-up time: O(1) ("Big-O of one", constant time, no matter how big the table grows). Worst-case time, in pathological situations: O(n) — but with a well-designed hash function, this is rare in practice.

Collisions happen when two different keys hash to the same slot. They are unavoidable on any finite table. Two industry-standard strategies handle them:

  • Chaining (separate chaining). Each slot stores a small linked list (or dynamic array) of all items that landed there. On lookup, you go to the slot and walk that short list. Easy to implement; tolerant of high load factors; the data structure can grow without re-hashing immediately.
  • Open Addressing (probing). If your slot is occupied, you try the next slot — and the next — until you find a free one. Several probing strategies exist (linear, quadratic, double-hashing). More memory-compact than chaining; performance degrades sharply once the table is more than ~70% full.

Where you meet hash tables every day: Python's dict, JavaScript's Map, Java's HashMap, C#'s Dictionary, Redis's main store, every DBMS (Database Management System) index that uses hashing, every browser cache, every login system.

3.2 Bloom Filters

A Bloom filter is a probabilistic data structure that answers exactly one question — "have I seen this item before?" — in constant time and tiny memory. The catch is in the precision:

  • "Definitely not" answers are 100% reliable. No false negatives, ever.
  • "Probably yes" answers are correct most of the time but may be false positives — the filter thinks it has seen the item when it actually has not.

The asymmetry is the entire point. A real Bloom filter uses a bit array of size m and k independent hash functions. To insert an item: hash it k ways, set the k corresponding bits to 1. To query: hash it k ways, check all k bits. If any bit is 0, the item is definitely absent. If all bits are 1, the item is probably present.

Real-world deployments:

  • Google Chrome Safe Browsing uses a Bloom-filter-like structure to flag dangerous URLs locally on the device without downloading a multi-gigabyte blacklist.
  • Cloudflare and Akamai drop known-bad traffic at the network edge using Bloom filters.
  • Bitcoin SPV (Simplified Payment Verification) wallets use them to privately query relevant transactions without revealing which addresses belong to them.
  • Anti-DDoS (Distributed Denial-of-Service) systems use them to recognise known attack signatures at line rate.

The trade-off — accept a small false-positive rate in exchange for orders-of-magnitude memory savings — is the deal at the heart of every production network defence stack.

3.3 Consistent Hashing

Plain hash-modulo-N distribution looks fine until you change N. Adding or removing one server changes the hash output for almost every key, which means almost every cache entry / session / record needs to move. On a planet-scale system, that is a recipe for outages.

Consistent hashing solves this. The idea, in one picture: imagine a circular ring. Each server is placed at some point on the ring (determined by hashing its name). Each key is also placed at some point on the ring (determined by hashing the key). Each key is served by the next server clockwise from its position.

The magic: when a server is added, only the keys between the new server and its clockwise neighbour need to move. Roughly 1/N of all keys, instead of all of them. When a server is removed, the same: only the keys that belonged to it migrate, and they go to one specific neighbour.

Real-world deployments:

  • Memcached and Redis Cluster distribute cached objects across many cache nodes.
  • Apache Cassandra distributes table partitions across cluster nodes using a consistent-hashing ring.
  • The architecture was popularised by Amazon's Dynamo paper (2007), which influenced almost every modern NoSQL system.
  • Load balancers from Cisco and HTTP-routing layers like NGINX use variants to keep user sessions sticky.

Most production implementations add virtual nodes — each physical server is placed at hundreds of positions on the ring — to smooth out load imbalances.

3.4 Graphs and How We Store Them

A graph G = (V, E) is a set of vertices (nodes) V connected by edges E. Graphs may be:

  • Directed (edges have a direction, like Twitter "follows") or undirected (edges have no direction, like Facebook "friendships").
  • Weighted (each edge carries a number — distance, cost, time) or unweighted.
  • Cyclic (contain at least one cycle) or acyclic (a DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) is the most common acyclic flavour).

Two storage formats matter for the exam:

Adjacency List. For each vertex, store a list of its direct neighbours. Memory cost: O(V + E). Best when the graph is sparse — that is, the number of edges is far smaller than V². Most real-world graphs (the Web, social networks, road networks) are sparse. Neighbour iteration is O(degree).

Adjacency Matrix. A V × V grid where cell (i, j) is 1 (or the edge weight) if vertices i and j are connected, 0 otherwise. Memory cost: O(V²). Edge-existence look-up is O(1). Best when the graph is dense (close to V² edges) or when you need fast pairwise look-ups (Floyd-Warshall, for example, naturally uses a matrix).

Rule of thumb to memorise:

  • Sparse and large → adjacency list.
  • Dense and small → adjacency matrix.

3.5 BFS and DFS — The Two Universal Graph Traversals

Breadth-First Search (BFS) explores level by level. It uses a queue (FIFO — First-In, First-Out). You add the starting node to the queue, then repeatedly dequeue a node, mark it as visited, and enqueue its unvisited neighbours.

Why BFS matters:

  • Finds the shortest path in an unweighted graph — guaranteed.
  • Used for "all nodes within K hops" questions (e.g., friend-of-friend on social networks).
  • Underlies network-discovery tools that scan a subnet level by level.

Depth-First Search (DFS) dives deep down a path before backtracking. It uses a stack (LIFO — Last-In, First-Out) — explicitly, or implicitly through recursion.

Why DFS matters:

  • Detects cycles in a graph.
  • Performs topological sort in a DAG.
  • Computes strongly connected components (with extensions like Tarjan's algorithm).
  • Walks dependency trees in build systems and package managers (npm, Maven, pip).

Time complexity for both: O(V + E) — you must touch every vertex and every edge at least once. Space complexity: O(V) for the visited-set plus the queue/stack.

A clean summary table:

Question Choose
Shortest path in an unweighted graph? BFS
All nodes within K hops? BFS
Cycle detection? DFS
Topological sort? DFS
Strongly connected components? DFS
Dependency-tree walk? DFS
Web-crawl style level-by-level? BFS

3.6 Network Topology Analysis

A practical, employment-relevant application of all of the above is network-topology discovery. The recipe combines several pieces of this chapter:

  1. Start at a known device, typically a core router.
  2. Use SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol) to ask each device "who are your neighbours?".
  3. Walk outward using BFS (most tools) or DFS (some niche cases), keeping a hash-table-based visited set to avoid re-visiting.
  4. The result is a graph in memory that the tool can render as a topology map.

Real tools that do this: LibreNMS, Zabbix, Nagios, SolarWinds NPM (Network Performance Monitor), and many commercial offerings from Cisco and Juniper Networks. When something breaks at 2 a.m., the topology map is what allows the on-call engineer to answer "what else does this break?" in seconds — a DFS-style transitive-closure query over the dependency graph.

This chapter pairs two contrasting moods. First, the beautiful classical algorithms that do solve shortest path optimally. Then the wall — problems for which no efficient algorithm has ever been found, and what working engineers do when they hit that wall.

4.1 Dijkstra's Algorithm

Invented by Edsger W. Dijkstra in 1956 and published in 1959, this is the single-source shortest-path algorithm. It is a greedy algorithm whose greediness is provably correct on graphs with non-negative edge weights.

How it works (in plain English):

  1. Set the source's tentative distance to 0 and every other vertex's tentative distance to infinity.
  2. Mark every vertex as unvisited.
  3. Repeatedly pick the unvisited vertex with the smallest tentative distance — this greedy pick is the algorithm's only piece of cleverness.
  4. Update each of its neighbours: if the route through this vertex is shorter than the neighbour's current tentative distance, replace it.
  5. Mark this vertex as visited and never touch it again.
  6. Stop when every reachable vertex is visited.

Time complexity: O((V + E) log V) with a binary heap (the standard implementation), or O(V²) with a plain array — the latter is acceptable for dense graphs.

Caveats:

  • Negative edge weights break Dijkstra. Use Bellman-Ford instead.
  • Returns shortest path from one source to everywhere. If you only need one destination, you can stop early — but the worst-case work is unchanged.
  • Inside Google Maps, Waze, Apple Maps, and every modern GPS (Global Positioning System), a Dijkstra-flavoured algorithm (often augmented with A*, contraction hierarchies, or other tricks) runs hundreds of times per minute.

4.2 Floyd-Warshall Algorithm

Published independently by Robert Floyd and Stephen Warshall in the early 1960s. It is the all-pairs shortest-path algorithm: it computes the shortest distance between every pair of vertices in one sweep, using Dynamic Programming.

The DP recurrence, in words: "Is the path from i to j shorter if I am allowed to use vertex k as a stop-over?" Iterate this question for every k from 1 to V, and the matrix converges to all-pairs shortest distances.

Time complexity: O(V³) ("Big-O of V cubed", cubic time). Acceptable for hundreds of vertices, painful for thousands, out of the question for millions.

When to use Floyd-Warshall vs running Dijkstra V times:

Aspect Dijkstra × V times Floyd-Warshall
Approach Run single-source SSSP from each vertex Single algorithm, one matrix
Time (sparse graphs) O(V × (V + E) log V) — faster O(V³)
Time (dense graphs) O(V³) (with heaps) O(V³) — equally fast
Negative weights? No (Dijkstra) Yes (Floyd-Warshall handles them)
Code length More Three nested loops — strikingly short
Best for Many sources, sparse graph All-pairs, dense graph, possible negative weights

Floyd-Warshall is alive and well today in airline-routing systems where the network has 100–500 hubs and "give me the cost from any hub to any other hub" is a frequent query.

4.3 Internet Routing — Where Shortest-Path Algorithms Live

Protocol Family Underlying algorithm Scope Standard

EIGRP (Enhanced Interior Gateway Routing Protocol) Distance-Vector hybrid Diffusing Update Algorithm Mid-sized enterprises Cisco-developed, now open: RFC 7868

Two big ideas to remember for the exam:

  • Distance-Vector = each router shouts its table to its neighbours; converges via Bellman-Ford-like updates; classic example is RIP.
  • Link-State = each router announces its links to everyone; each router builds the same full map and runs Dijkstra locally; classic example is OSPF.

BGP is the special one. It is not about geometric shortest path; it is about policy. It is the protocol that holds the Internet together between organisations like Telefónica, Bell Canada, and every other transit provider. BGP misconfigurations are the cause of most of the famous "the Internet broke for an hour" events — including the October 2021 Facebook outage and numerous incidents documented at Cloudflare's engineering blog.

4.4 Computational Intractability — When No Efficient Algorithm Exists

So far, every algorithm in this course has had a polynomial running time — O(n), O(n log n), O(n²), O(n³). Polynomial time is what computer scientists informally call tractable: even for big inputs, the work grows in a way the universe can finish.

But there exists a famous family of problems for which the best known algorithms take exponential time, written O(2ⁿ) ("Big-O of 2 to the n") or worse, like O(n!) ("Big-O of n factorial"). These are intractable. The numbers get out of hand fast:

The exponential and factorial columns leave the building well before 30 items. This is not a numerical curiosity; it is a wall every working engineer hits.

Three canonical intractable problems you should know by name:

  • Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP). Visit N cities exactly once and return home. Find the shortest tour. Used in delivery routing at Glovo, Amazon, and Canada Post.
  • Boolean Satisfiability (SAT, and 3-SAT). Given a logical formula, is there an assignment to its variables that makes it true? Used in hardware verification at Intel and AMD.
  • 0/1 Knapsack Problem (decision version). Given items with weights and values and a capacity, can you reach value V? Used in cargo loading at Iberia Cargo and Air Canada Cargo.

4.5 NP-Completeness — Cook, Levin, and the One-Million-Dollar Question

In 1971, Stephen Cook at the University of Toronto proved a result that earned him the 1982 Turing Award: thousands of problems that look completely different are all the same problem in disguise. Find an efficient algorithm for any one of them and you have efficient algorithms for all of them. Prove one of them fundamentally hard and you have proved all of them fundamentally hard. The same result was found independently by Leonid Levin in the USSR (Union of Soviet Socialist Republics) at almost the same time.

This club of equivalent problems is called NP-complete. NP stands for Non-deterministic Polynomial time and informally means "the answer can be verified quickly, even if finding the answer is hard".

This led to the most famous open question in computer science: does P = NP? That is, is the easy class P (Polynomial time) actually equal to the bigger class NP? The Clay Mathematics Institute offers a one million US dollars prize for a proof either way. After fifty-plus years, nobody has claimed it.

4.6 What Engineers Actually Do — Approximations and Heuristics

When you meet an NP-complete problem in production, you have three honest options:

  • Brute force. Try every possibility. Acceptable when the input is very small — fewer than ~20 items, perhaps 25 if you can wait.
  • Approximation algorithm. A polynomial-time algorithm with a provable guarantee on how far from optimal it can be — for example, "my answer is at most 1.5 times the optimum". These are the gold standard of theoretical CS applied to industry.
  • Heuristic. A polynomial-time algorithm that usually produces good answers but offers no formal guarantee. Often what production systems actually run.

The famous Nearest-Neighbour heuristic for TSP is the simplest example: "start anywhere, then always go to the nearest unvisited city, finally return home". On random TSP instances it produces a tour roughly 25% longer than optimal. That is bad enough to fail a maths exam but good enough to deliver paella across Madrid before it cools.

Smarter approximations exist:

  • The Christofides algorithm (1976) guarantees a tour at most 1.5× the optimum for metric TSP — a famous theoretical result.
  • The Lin-Kernighan heuristic regularly produces tours within 1% of optimum on real-world data.
  • The Concorde and LKH solvers — developed at the University of Waterloo and at Roskilde University respectively — can solve real-world TSP instances with thousands of cities to provable optimality.

The engineer's job is to know which of brute force, approximation, or heuristic to reach for. That choice is the difference between an engineer who reads textbooks and one who ships systems.

Covers: Chapter 1 — Sequential vs Binary Search.

Scenario. A bakery in Lavapiés, Madrid, keeps a printed loyalty list of its 200 customers. The owner asks you two separate questions on the same day:

  1. "Is María García on the list?" The list is sorted alphabetically.
  2. "Is the customer with the phone number +34 600 123 456 on the list?" The list is sorted alphabetically by name, not by phone number.

For each question, recommend a search algorithm and justify your choice.

Model Answer. Question 1: the list is sorted by name, the search key (a name) matches the sort order, so binary search is the right tool. With 200 customers, binary search needs at most ⌈log₂(200)⌉ ≈ 8 comparisons, against an average of 100 for sequential search.

Question 2: the list is sorted by name, but the search key is a phone number — the sort order is useless for this query. The only correct choice is sequential search (also called linear search): walk the list and compare each customer's phone number. Average and worst case both O(n) = up to 200 comparisons. The lesson: binary search is only available when the data is sorted by the same field you are searching on. Sorting the list a second time by phone number would help if the owner expects many phone-number look-ups; for a one-off question, sequential search is cheaper than the sort itself.

Covers: Chapter 1 — Greedy Algorithm Strategy.

Scenario. You are attending a one-day technology conference in Toronto. There are nine talks, each with a fixed start and end time. You can attend at most one talk at any given moment — you want to attend as many non-overlapping talks as possible. The schedule is:

Talk Start End
A 09:00 10:30
B 09:30 10:00
C 10:00 11:00
D 10:30 12:00
E 11:00 12:30
F 12:30 13:30
G 13:00 15:00
H 14:00 16:00
I 16:00 17:00

Describe an algorithm, run it on this data, and state its time complexity.

Model Answer. This is the Activity Selection Problem, the textbook example where a greedy strategy is provably optimal. The greedy rule: sort all talks by end time, then iterate through them, picking each talk that starts no earlier than the end of the previously selected talk.

Sorted by end time: B (09:30→10:00), C (10:00→11:00), A (09:00→10:30), E (11:00→12:30), D (10:30→12:00), F (12:30→13:30), G (13:00→15:00), H (14:00→16:00), I (16:00→17:00).

Greedy pass:

  1. Pick B (ends 10:00). last_end = 10:00.
  2. C starts at 10:00 ≥ 10:00 → pick C (ends 11:00). last_end = 11:00.
  3. A starts at 09:00 < 11:00 → skip.
  4. E starts at 11:00 ≥ 11:00 → pick E (ends 12:30). last_end = 12:30.
  5. D starts at 10:30 < 12:30 → skip.
  6. F starts at 12:30 ≥ 12:30 → pick F (ends 13:30). last_end = 13:30.
  7. G starts at 13:00 < 13:30 → skip.
  8. H starts at 14:00 ≥ 13:30 → pick H (ends 16:00). last_end = 16:00.
  9. I starts at 16:00 ≥ 16:00 → pick I (ends 17:00).

Final selection: {B, C, E, F, H, I} = 6 talks, the maximum possible. Time complexity: O(n log n) dominated by the sort; the sweep itself is O(n). Why greedy works here: the problem has both the greedy choice property (the earliest-ending talk is always part of some optimal solution) and optimal substructure (after picking it, the sub-problem on the remaining talks is the same problem).

Covers: Chapter 2 — Dynamic Programming (Memoization vs Tabulation).

Scenario. A hotel in Salamanca is offering a quirky promotion: each step of its grand staircase has a different toll, and guests get a discount based on the minimum total toll they pay to reach the top. From any step you can climb either 1 step or 2 steps at a time, and you start from the ground (before step 0). The 6 step tolls in euros, in order, are: [3, 1, 5, 2, 8, 4].

(a) Define the DP (Dynamic Programming) recurrence. (b) Compute the minimum cost using tabulation. (c) Would memoization have been better here? Why or why not?

Model Answer. (a) Let cost[i] denote the toll of step i (0-indexed) and dp[i] the minimum total toll to reach step i. The recurrence is dp[i] = cost[i] + min(dp[i-1], dp[i-2]), with base cases dp[0] = cost[0] = 3 and dp[1] = cost[1] = 1 (assuming you can start your first jump as either 1-step or 2-step from the ground). The final answer is min(dp[n-1], dp[n-2]) — you can step off the top from either of the last two steps.

(b) Tabulation:

i cost[i] dp[i] = cost[i] + min(dp[i-1], dp[i-2])
0 3 3
1 1 1
2 5 5 + min(1, 3) = 6
3 2 2 + min(6, 1) = 3
4 8 8 + min(3, 6) = 11
5 4 4 + min(11, 3) = 7

Final answer: min(dp[4], dp[5]) = min(11, 7) = €7.

(c) Tabulation is the better fit here. The problem requires every sub-problem to be solved (you cannot reach step 5 without knowing dp[4] and dp[3]) and the iteration order is trivially "left to right". Memoization would yield the same result but pay extra for recursion overhead and risk a stack overflow on much larger staircases. Memoization shines when only a subset of sub-problems is actually needed.

Covers: Chapter 1 (Greedy) + Chapter 2 (DP).

Scenario. A fictional country uses coin denominations {1, 3, 4} cents and never issued 2-cent or 5-cent coins. A merchant must give back exactly 6 cents in change using the fewest coins possible.

(a) What answer does a greedy algorithm (always take the largest coin that fits) produce? (b) What is the true optimum? (c) Which technique would correctly solve the general coin-change problem on any denomination set, and what is its complexity?

Model Answer. (a) Greedy takes the largest coin that does not exceed the remaining amount: 6 → take 4 → 2 → take 1 → 1 → take 1 → 0. That is {4, 1, 1} = 3 coins.

(b) The true optimum is {3, 3} = 2 coins. Greedy missed it because the locally best first move (taking 4) left a remainder (2) that could not be made from any combination of two coins — only a 1+1 pair, costing one extra coin overall.

(c) The general coin-change problem (minimum number of coins to make a target) is a classic Dynamic Programming problem. Recurrence: dp[v] = 1 + min(dp[v - c] for c in coin_set if c ≤ v), with dp[0] = 0. Time complexity: O(V × |coins|) where V is the target value and |coins| is the number of denominations — pseudo-polynomial in V. This is the kind of problem the 0/1 Knapsack and Bellman-Ford algorithms all sit in the same family with. Lesson: greedy is fast, but unless you can prove the greedy-choice property holds for your coin set, default to DP.

Covers: Chapter 3 — Hash Tables and Collisions.

Scenario. A startup in Montréal is designing the back-end for a chat application. They expect 10 million registered usernames and need an in-memory cache that supports two operations: is_username_taken(name) and register_username(name), both expected to run in microseconds.

(a) Which data structure should they choose, and why? (b) Compare the two main collision-handling strategies for that data structure. (c) Roughly how much memory should they budget, in megabytes, if each username averages 16 bytes and each cache entry costs an extra 40 bytes of bookkeeping?

Model Answer. (a) The right structure is a hash table (a Python dict, Java HashMap, or — at scale — a distributed cache like Redis or Memcached). Average-case insertion and lookup are O(1), independent of the number of stored items. With a well-chosen hash function and a reasonable load factor (say, 0.7), the expected number of probes per lookup is below 2.

(b) Chaining (separate chaining): each slot holds a small linked list (or dynamic array) of collisions. Pros: simple, tolerant of high load factors (can exceed 1.0), graceful degradation. Cons: pointer-chasing hurts CPU cache performance. Open addressing (probing): collisions are resolved by probing the next slot (linear) or by a different hash (double hashing). Pros: better CPU-cache locality, more memory-compact. Cons: performance collapses past ~70% load, and deletion requires "tombstone" markers. For a startup that expects steady growth and wants simple operational behaviour, chaining is usually the safer choice.

(c) Per username: 16 bytes (name) + 40 bytes (bookkeeping) = 56 bytes per entry. For 10 million entries: 10 000 000 × 56 bytes = 560 000 000 bytes ≈ 534 MB (megabytes). The cache should comfortably fit on a single modern server with 1 GB of RAM (Random-Access Memory) headroom, though for resilience the team should still shard across nodes using consistent hashing (Section 3.3).

Covers: Chapter 3 — Bloom Filters.

Scenario. A small Canadian Internet Service Provider (ISP) wants to flag connections to known phishing URLs (Uniform Resource Locators) at the gateway. The phishing-URL list, distributed daily by Google Safe Browsing, contains 3 million entries. The ISP cannot afford to load the full list into the gateway's fast memory.

(a) Justify why a Bloom filter is a suitable data structure for this problem. (b) What is the correct response to a "definitely no" result, and what is the correct response to a "probably yes" result? (c) What is the consequence of a false positive in this deployment, and is it acceptable?

Model Answer. (a) A Bloom filter is ideal because (i) the dataset is huge and read-only, (ii) the gateway has hard memory constraints, (iii) the operation needed is membership testing only, and (iv) false negatives are unacceptable — letting a phishing URL through silently — while false positives are tolerable because they can be verified by a slower authoritative check. A Bloom filter sized for 3 million items and a 1% false-positive rate needs roughly 3.6 MB of memory — easily resident in the gateway's L3 cache or fast RAM. Compare this to the multi-gigabyte alternative of storing every URL outright.

(b) "Definitely no" means none of the k hash positions for the URL are set; the URL is guaranteed absent from the phishing list, so the connection passes through immediately. "Probably yes" means all k positions are set; the URL may or may not be in the list, so the gateway performs a slower authoritative lookup against the full list (kept on disk or queried from an upstream service like Cloudflare's threat database).

(c) A false positive triggers an unnecessary slower lookup — extra latency on that specific connection, but no security risk: the authoritative check will correctly identify the URL as clean and let the connection through after the brief delay. Yes, this is fully acceptable for the ISP's use case. Critically, a Bloom filter cannot produce a false negative by design — so no real phishing URL will ever silently slip past. That asymmetry is exactly what makes Bloom filters the dominant choice for first-pass network defence at companies like Cloudflare and Akamai.

Covers: Chapter 3 — BFS and Network Topology.

Scenario. A network engineer at a hospital in Madrid is mapping the IT (Information Technology) infrastructure. Starting at the core router R0, the discovery tool needs to find every device on the network and report the minimum number of hops from R0 to each device. The topology is:

  • R0 connects to switches S1 and S2.
  • S1 connects to printers P1, P2 and to switch S3.
  • S2 connects to laptops L1, L2.
  • S3 connects to a NAS (Network-Attached Storage) device N1 and to a server Srv1.

(a) Which traversal algorithm should the tool use, and why? (b) Give the visit order from R0. (c) State the minimum-hop distance from R0 to each device.

(a) BFS (Breadth-First Search) is the correct choice because (i) the graph is unweighted (each link is one hop, all equally costly), (ii) the engineer asks for minimum hop count to each device, and BFS gives shortest paths in unweighted graphs by construction. Covers: Chapter 4 — Dijkstra's Algorithm.

Scenario. A traveller at Sol (Puerta del Sol metro station, Madrid) needs the fastest evening route to Aeropuerto T4 (Barajas Airport, Terminal 4). The simplified graph of metro segments has these travel times in minutes:

  • Sol → Gran Vía: 2
  • Gran Vía → Avenida de América: 8
  • Sol → Atocha: 4
  • Atocha → Nuevos Ministerios: 7
  • Gran Vía → Nuevos Ministerios: 6
  • Avenida de América → Aeropuerto T4: 15
  • Nuevos Ministerios → Aeropuerto T4: 17

(a) Trace Dijkstra's algorithm step by step. (b) Give the shortest travel time from Sol to Aeropuerto T4 and the route. (c) Could Bellman-Ford have given a different answer here?

Model Answer. (a) Initial state: distances {Sol:0, GV:∞, AA:∞, At:∞, NM:∞, T4:∞}. All vertices unvisited.

Step Pick (smallest unvisited) Updates
1 Sol (dist 0) GV: 2 (via Sol), At: 4 (via Sol)
2 Gran Vía (dist 2) AA: 10 (via GV), NM: 8 (via GV)
3 Atocha (dist 4) NM: min(8, 4+7)=8 — no change
4 Nuevos Ministerios (dist 8) T4: 25 (via NM)
5 Avenida de América (dist 10) T4: min(25, 10+15)=25 — no change
6 Aeropuerto T4 (dist 25) —

(b) Shortest travel time: 25 minutes. Two equally optimal routes exist: Sol → Gran Vía → Nuevos Ministerios → T4, and Sol → Gran Vía → Avenida de América → T4 (both 2 + 6 + 17 = 25 and 2 + 8 + 15 = 25). Dijkstra finds the minimum cost; multiple routes can achieve it.

(c) No. All edge weights are non-negative — they represent travel times — so Dijkstra is correct and Bellman-Ford would have given the same answer. Bellman-Ford would only be necessary if some segments had negative weights, which is meaningless for time. The trade-off: Bellman-Ford runs in O(V × E) versus Dijkstra's O((V + E) log V) with a binary heap. For a real Madrid metro graph (≈ 300 stations, ≈ 700 segments), the difference matters; for this tiny example, both are instantaneous.

Covers: Chapter 4 — TSP, Intractability, Approximation.

Scenario. A last-mile courier company operating in Toronto has 100 deliveries to make in the next 3 hours, starting and ending at the depot. The dispatcher has a TSP (Travelling Salesman Problem) solver but must decide between three options:

  1. Brute-force enumeration of all tours.
  2. The Nearest-Neighbour heuristic.
  3. The Lin-Kernighan algorithm with a 30-second time budget.

(a) Estimate how long brute-force would take, and whether it is feasible. (b) Compare the expected tour quality of options 2 and 3. (c) Which option should the dispatcher choose in production, and why?

Model Answer. (a) Brute-force checks all permutations of stops. For 100 stops with a fixed depot, that is 99! tours ≈ 9.3 × 10¹⁵⁵ — a 156-digit number. Even at a trillion tours per second on the fastest hardware ever built, finishing would take more time than the age of the universe (≈ 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years). Infeasible by every conceivable margin.

(b) Nearest-Neighbour runs in O(n²) time — well under a millisecond for 100 stops — and on random TSP instances produces tours roughly 25% worse than optimal on average, occasionally much worse on adversarial inputs. Lin-Kernighan, even with a 30-second time budget, regularly produces tours within 1% of optimal on real-world data; it iteratively applies 2-opt and 3-opt edge swaps to refine an initial tour.

(c) The dispatcher should choose option 3 (Lin-Kernighan). Reasoning: (i) the 30-second budget is acceptable when the operational window is 3 hours, (ii) saving ~24% of tour length translates directly into fuel cost, courier hours, and customer wait time, (iii) Lin-Kernighan's bound on real-world data is dramatically better than Nearest-Neighbour's. Nearest-Neighbour is the right pick only if you have milliseconds and not seconds — for example, inside a real-time replanning loop after a courier is delayed. Brute force is for academic illustration only at this input size. This trade-off — accept 1% sub-optimality for tractable runtime — is the daily reality at companies like Glovo, Amazon, and Canada Post.

Covers: Chapter 3 — Consistent Hashing.

Scenario. A streaming service operating in the EU (European Union) and Canada runs a 20-node distributed cache. Each user session is pinned to one cache node. The team plans to add a 21st node to handle a traffic spike during a major football match.

(a) Without consistent hashing — i.e. using plain hash(user_id) mod N — what proportion of users have their cache entries moved when N changes from 20 to 21? (b) With consistent hashing on a virtual ring, what proportion moves? (c) Why does the team use virtual nodes, and what trade-off does that introduce?

Model Answer. (a) With plain modulo, nearly every user is re-mapped. The slot assignment changes from user_id mod 20 to user_id mod 21; for almost every user_id, those two values are different. Practically, ~20/21 ≈ 95% of users lose their cache locality. Their next request becomes a cache miss; if many users miss simultaneously, the upstream database is hit by a "thundering herd" and the system can collapse — a real failure mode known as a cache stampede.

(b) With consistent hashing on a ring, only the users whose ring positions fall in the arc between the new node and its clockwise neighbour are moved. On a uniform ring with 21 nodes, that arc is on average 1/21 of the ring — so about 1/21 ≈ 4.8% of users are re-mapped. The other 95% keep their cache locality untouched. The database experiences only a small, predictable refresh load.

(c) Virtual nodes (each physical node appearing at, say, 100 random positions on the ring) smooth out load imbalance. With only 1 ring position per node, random hash placement often produces hot spots — one node ending up with twice the traffic of another. With 100 virtual positions per physical node, the law of large numbers averages the load across the ring. Trade-off: the ring becomes larger (2000 positions instead of 20), look-ups take marginally longer (O(log V) where V is the number of virtual positions), and metadata grows. In production, this trade-off is virtually always worth it. The original Amazon Dynamo paper (2007) discusses these design choices in detail.

MCQ 1. (Chapter 1 — Sequential Search) What is the worst-case time complexity of Sequential Search on a list of n items?

a) O(1) b) O(log n) c) O(n) ✓ d) O(n log n)

Correct answer: c) O(n) — In the worst case the target is either the last element or absent entirely, forcing the algorithm to compare against every one of the n items. The other options describe different complexity classes that Sequential Search never matches.

MCQ 2. (Chapter 1 — Binary Search) Which of the following is a mandatory pre-condition for Binary Search to give correct results?

a) The data must contain unique values. b) The data must be sorted in monotonic order. ✓ c) The data must be stored in a hash table. d) The data must contain at least 1 000 items.

Correct answer: b) — Binary Search relies on the invariant that values to the left of a position are ≤ the value at that position (and vice versa). Without sorted data, the algorithm runs happily but returns wrong answers. Duplicates, hashing, and minimum input size are all irrelevant to its correctness.

MCQ 3. (Chapter 1 — Binary Search) On a sorted array of 1 048 576 items, the maximum number of comparisons Binary Search will perform is approximately:

a) 10 b) 20 ✓ c) 1 024 d) 1 048 576

Correct answer: b) 20 — Binary Search performs at most ⌈log₂(n) + 1⌉ comparisons. log₂(1 048 576) = 20, since 2²⁰ = 1 048 576. Doubling the input adds exactly one extra step — the headline property of logarithmic algorithms.

MCQ 4. (Chapter 1 — Sequential vs Binary) A small embedded device must search an unsorted list of 25 sensor readings exactly once per minute. Which search is preferable and why?

a) Binary Search — always faster. b) Sequential Search — sorting first would cost more than the search itself. ✓ c) Hash table lookup — only correct option. d) Either choice is equally good; the difference disappears below 1 000 items.

Correct answer: b) — Sorting 25 items costs O(n log n) ≈ 116 operations, while Sequential Search on the unsorted list costs at most 25 comparisons. On a one-off search, the sort would dominate the total work. Binary Search would also fail outright if used on unsorted data without first sorting it.

MCQ 5. (Chapter 1 — Greedy) Which of the following classical algorithms is NOT a greedy algorithm?

a) Dijkstra's Algorithm. b) Kruskal's Algorithm for Minimum Spanning Tree. c) Bellman-Ford Algorithm. ✓ d) Huffman Coding for compression.

Correct answer: c) — Bellman-Ford is a Dynamic Programming algorithm — it iteratively relaxes every edge V−1 times. Dijkstra, Kruskal, and Huffman are all greedy algorithms that commit to the locally optimal pick at each step and never revisit the decision.

MCQ 6. (Chapter 1 — Greedy) For a greedy algorithm to guarantee the globally optimal solution, the problem must possess two properties: optimal substructure and …

a) Overlapping subproblems. b) The greedy choice property. ✓ c) Polynomial-time verification. d) A unique solution.

Correct answer: b) — The greedy choice property says that the locally optimal pick at each step is always part of some globally optimal solution. Without it, greedy can construct a sub-optimal answer (the {1, 3, 4}-cent coin-change counter-example demonstrates this).

MCQ 7. (Chapter 1 — Greedy fails) The Activity Selection Problem (maximise non-overlapping activities) is solved optimally by a greedy algorithm. Which greedy criterion is correct?

a) Always pick the activity that starts earliest. b) Always pick the activity with the shortest duration. c) Always pick the activity that ends earliest. ✓ d) Always pick the activity with the most overlaps to remove conflicts.

Correct answer: c) — Sorting by end time and always picking the earliest-ending activity that does not conflict with the previously chosen one is the only greedy rule that is provably optimal for Activity Selection. Picking by start time or by duration is easy to refute with a counter-example.

MCQ 8. (Chapter 1 — Greedy + Coin Change) For the coin denominations {1, 5, 10, 25} and a target of 30 cents, the greedy algorithm returns…

a) 1 coin of value 30. b) 2 coins: {25, 5}. ✓ c) 3 coins: {10, 10, 10}. d) 6 coins: {5, 5, 5, 5, 5, 5}.

Correct answer: b) — Greedy takes the largest coin that does not exceed the remaining amount: 30 → take 25 → 5 → take 5 → 0. Two coins. The US (United States) coin set is canonical, so greedy here happens to give the same answer as DP.

MCQ 9. (Chapter 1 — Binary Search subtle point) Inside Binary Search, computing the midpoint as mid = (low + high) / 2 is dangerous in some languages because…

a) Division is undefined for sorted lists. b) The sum low + high can overflow the integer type for very large arrays. ✓ c) The compiler may optimise it to floating point and lose precision. d) The CPU executes integer division too slowly to use it inside a loop.

Correct answer: b) — This is the famous bug pointed out by Joshua Bloch in his Google Research blog post (2006). For arrays larger than 2³⁰ items in 32-bit signed arithmetic, low + high overflows to a negative number. The safe form is mid = low + (high - low) / 2.

MCQ 10. (Chapter 1 — Sequential Search) On a list whose first element is the target, Sequential Search runs in:

a) O(1) ✓ b) O(log n) c) O(n) d) O(n²)

Correct answer: a) O(1) — This is the best case of Sequential Search: the very first comparison succeeds and the algorithm returns immediately. The average and worst case remain O(n).

MCQ 11. (Chapter 1 — Greedy intuition) Which problem is the textbook example where a greedy algorithm produces a sub-optimal solution?

a) Activity Selection. b) Huffman Coding. c) Minimum Spanning Tree. d) 0/1 Knapsack Problem. ✓

Correct answer: d) — In the 0/1 Knapsack problem (items cannot be split), greedy strategies (pick by highest value, by highest value-to-weight ratio, etc.) can all be defeated by carefully constructed inputs. The optimal solution requires Dynamic Programming with time complexity O(n × W). The fractional knapsack (items can be split) is the version where greedy by value-to-weight ratio is provably optimal — but that is a different problem.

MCQ 12. (Chapter 2 — DP basics) Dynamic Programming is applicable when a problem has both overlapping subproblems and…

a) Disjoint subproblems. b) Optimal substructure. ✓ c) Linear recurrences only. d) Constant time per operation.

Correct answer: b) — A problem must have optimal substructure (the optimal solution to the whole contains optimal solutions to its parts) and overlapping subproblems for DP to be both correct and beneficial.

MCQ 13. (Chapter 2 — Memoization vs Tabulation) Which statement about memoization is true?

a) It iterates from base cases upward. b) It is a top-down approach using a cache to store already-computed results. ✓ c) It never uses recursion. d) It is always strictly faster than tabulation in practice.

Correct answer: b) — Memoization is the top-down flavour of DP: write the natural recursive solution, add a cache. Tabulation is the bottom-up flavour. Memoization can be slower than tabulation in practice because of recursion overhead and risks stack overflow.

MCQ 14. (Chapter 2 — Fibonacci) Computing the n-th Fibonacci number with a naive recursive algorithm has time complexity:

a) O(n) b) O(n log n) c) O(2ⁿ) ✓ d) O(n²)

Correct answer: c) O(2ⁿ) — The naive recursion branches into two calls at every level, building an exponential call tree. DP — either memoization or tabulation — reduces this to O(n) with O(n) space, or O(n) with O(1) space using a two-variable rolling window.

MCQ 15. (Chapter 2 — Bellman-Ford) The Bellman-Ford algorithm is best classified as…

a) A greedy algorithm. b) A divide-and-conquer algorithm. c) A Dynamic Programming algorithm. ✓ d) A randomised algorithm.

Correct answer: c) — Bellman-Ford iteratively relaxes every edge in the graph, building up shortest-path estimates one round at a time. It is a textbook tabulation-style DP applied to the single-source shortest-path problem.

MCQ 16. (Chapter 2 — Knapsack) The 0/1 Knapsack problem has time complexity (with DP) of:

a) O(n) b) O(n log n) c) O(n × W) where n is the number of items and W is the capacity ✓ d) O(2ⁿ)

Correct answer: c) — The DP table has n rows and W columns; each cell is computed in O(1). This is pseudo-polynomial because W is the value of the capacity, not its size in bits. If W is exponentially large in its bit-representation, this can still be slow — which is one of the reasons Knapsack is NP-hard in the strict complexity-theoretic sense.

MCQ 17. (Chapter 2 — Optimal Substructure) Which problem does NOT have optimal substructure?

a) Shortest path between two cities. b) Longest simple path between two cities. ✓ c) Matrix chain multiplication. d) Longest Common Subsequence (LCS).

Correct answer: b) — The longest simple path problem lacks optimal substructure because the "no node revisit" constraint means a longer A-to-B sub-path can block the B-to-C sub-path. This is why finding longest simple paths is NP-hard, while finding shortest paths is polynomial.

MCQ 18. (Chapter 2 — Memoization risk) A potential issue with using memoization on a problem requiring deep recursion is…

a) Incorrect results due to caching. b) Stack overflow due to excessive recursion depth. ✓ c) Inability to store integer values. d) Failure to terminate on cyclic inputs.

Correct answer: b) — Each recursive call consumes a stack frame. On problems with deep call chains (say, a recurrence over an array of length 10⁶), the call stack may overflow. Tabulation avoids this risk entirely because it is purely iterative.

MCQ 19. (Chapter 2 — DP applications) Which of the following is a real-world application of Dynamic Programming?

a) Sorting unsorted arrays. b) Generating cryptographic keys. c) DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) sequence alignment at the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information). ✓ d) Allocating IP (Internet Protocol) addresses in a subnet.

Correct answer: c) — Sequence alignment uses algorithms like Needleman-Wunsch and Smith-Waterman, both DP-based, to find optimal alignments between genetic sequences. These tools are foundational at NCBI and in tools like BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool). The other three tasks do not use DP.

MCQ 20. (Chapter 2 — Tabulation) Tabulation is generally preferred over memoization when…

a) Only a small subset of subproblems is needed. b) The recurrence is non-deterministic. c) All subproblems must be solved and recursion depth is a concern. ✓ d) The base case is unknown.

Correct answer: c) — Tabulation eagerly computes every subproblem in a known order, eliminates recursion overhead, and avoids stack-overflow risk. It pays only when those sub-problems are actually needed; if you would only have asked for a small fraction of them, memoization is leaner.

MCQ 21. (Chapter 2 — QoS Routing) QoS (Quality of Service) routing in modern networks differs from basic shortest-path routing because it…

a) Uses only Bellman-Ford and never Dijkstra. b) Considers multiple constraints such as minimum bandwidth and maximum delay simultaneously. ✓ c) Is always solved by greedy algorithms. d) Ignores the network topology and uses static tables only.

Correct answer: b) — QoS routing extends shortest-path DP to multi-constraint optimisation. The IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force) documented these requirements in RFC 2386. For example, a video call might require ≥ 10 Mbps (Megabits per second) of bandwidth and ≤ 100 ms of delay — both constraints must be satisfied simultaneously.

MCQ 22. (Chapter 2 — Greedy vs DP) Which statement best contrasts Greedy with Dynamic Programming?

a) Greedy guarantees optimality on all problems; DP does not. b) DP is always faster than Greedy. c) Greedy commits to one choice without reconsidering, while DP explores subproblems and combines optimal solutions. ✓ d) Greedy uses memoization; DP uses tabulation.

Correct answer: c) — This is the cleanest one-sentence contrast. Greedy is fast but only correct when the greedy-choice property holds. DP is more memory-intensive but works on any problem with optimal substructure and overlapping subproblems.

MCQ 23. (Chapter 3 — Hash tables) The expected time complexity of a successful lookup in a hash table is:

a) O(log n) b) O(1) on average. ✓ c) O(n) always. d) O(n log n).

Correct answer: b) — With a well-distributed hash function and a sensible load factor, expected lookup time is constant, independent of the number of stored items. Worst-case time can degrade to O(n) under adversarial inputs that produce many collisions, which is why production code uses cryptographic or randomised hash families.

MCQ 24. (Chapter 3 — Collisions) In separate chaining, a collision is handled by…

a) Replacing the existing element with the new one. b) Storing both items in a linked list (or dynamic array) at the colliding slot. ✓ c) Resizing the hash table immediately after every collision. d) Refusing to insert the new item.

Correct answer: b) — Separate chaining stores all items mapped to the same slot in a small list rooted at that slot. Lookup walks the chain. Open addressing (probing), the alternative strategy, instead finds the next free slot.

MCQ 25. (Chapter 3 — Bloom Filter) A Bloom filter's defining trade-off is that…

a) It is slower than a hash table for lookups. b) It may produce false negatives but not false positives. c) It may produce false positives but never false negatives. ✓ d) It uses more memory than a regular set but offers exact answers.

Correct answer: c) — A Bloom filter answers "definitely not" with full certainty and "probably yes" with a tunable false-positive rate. No real member is ever missed. This asymmetric guarantee is what makes Bloom filters useful at the edge of networks — at companies like Cloudflare and Akamai — for high-throughput membership filtering.

MCQ 26. (Chapter 3 — Bloom Filter applications) Google Chrome Safe Browsing uses a Bloom-filter-like structure primarily to…

a) Compress all websites' content. b) Detect known malicious URLs (Uniform Resource Locators) without downloading the entire blacklist locally. ✓ c) Encrypt user passwords. d) Generate random session tokens.

Correct answer: b) — Chrome must answer "is this URL on the malicious-sites list?" fast and with tiny memory; downloading the full list would consume gigabytes. A Bloom-filter-like structure provides O(1) membership tests in a few megabytes, with false positives resolved by a slower authoritative lookup.

MCQ 27. (Chapter 3 — Consistent Hashing) Consistent hashing is the preferred strategy for distributing keys across N servers because…

a) It guarantees zero collisions on every key. b) When N changes by 1, only ~1/N of the keys need to be remapped. ✓ c) It works only on sorted data. d) It eliminates the need for any hash function.

Correct answer: b) — Consistent hashing places keys and servers on a ring; only the slice between the added/removed server and its clockwise neighbour is affected. Plain hash mod N would re-map ~95% of keys when N changes — catastrophic at scale. The Amazon Dynamo paper (2007) made this approach famous.

MCQ 28. (Chapter 3 — Graph representation) For a sparse graph with millions of nodes and only a few connections per node, the preferred representation is…

a) Adjacency list. ✓ b) Adjacency matrix. c) Hash table only. d) A single sorted array.

Correct answer: a) — An adjacency list costs O(V + E) memory, ideal for sparse graphs. An adjacency matrix would cost O(V²) — for one million nodes that is one trillion cells, almost all of them zeros. The matrix is preferable only for dense graphs or when O(1) edge-existence lookup is essential.

MCQ 29. (Chapter 3 — BFS) Which data structure does Breadth-First Search use for its frontier?

a) A stack (LIFO — Last-In, First-Out). b) A priority queue. c) A queue (FIFO — First-In, First-Out). ✓ d) A hash set with random eviction.

Correct answer: c) — BFS visits the graph in level order; the FIFO discipline of a queue is exactly what produces level-by-level exploration. Stacks turn BFS into DFS; priority queues turn it into Dijkstra.

MCQ 30. (Chapter 3 — DFS) DFS is the natural choice for which of the following tasks?

a) Finding the shortest path in an unweighted graph. b) Detecting cycles in a directed graph. ✓ c) Computing the diameter of a tree in one pass. d) Pinging every device on a subnet level by level.

Correct answer: b) — DFS maintains a "currently-on-the-stack" set; encountering an already-on-stack vertex during exploration is a cycle. BFS does not naturally distinguish this. BFS is the right pick for unweighted shortest paths and for level-by-level network discovery.

MCQ 31. (Chapter 3 — Graph traversal complexity) The time complexity of traversing a graph with BFS or DFS is:

a) O(V) b) O(E) c) O(V + E) ✓ d) O(V²)

Correct answer: c) — Both algorithms must visit every vertex (O(V) work) and every edge (O(E) work) at most a constant number of times. The sum, O(V + E), is asymptotically optimal — no graph traversal can do better.

MCQ 32. (Chapter 3 — Topology discovery) A network-discovery tool starts at a known router and lists every device level by level. Which combination of techniques is most natural?

a) Sequential search + adjacency matrix. b) Binary search + DFS. c) SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol) + BFS + hash-set of visited devices. ✓ d) Dijkstra's algorithm + Bloom filter.

Correct answer: c) — Real tools like LibreNMS, Zabbix, and SolarWinds NPM (Network Performance Monitor) query each device over SNMP, traverse using BFS for level-ordered exploration, and use a hash-set to avoid revisiting loops. Dijkstra is overkill for unweighted hop counts; Bloom filters are not needed for the modest number of devices on a corporate network.

MCQ 33. (Chapter 3 — Hash function essentials) A "good" hash function for a hash table must, at minimum, be…

a) Slow but cryptographically secure. b) Deterministic and distribute keys reasonably uniformly across slots. ✓ c) Sensitive to system time so it produces different values on each run. d) Reversible — able to recover the input from the hash.

Correct answer: b) — Determinism is non-negotiable: the same key must always hash to the same slot. Uniform distribution keeps collisions rare. Reversibility is the opposite of what we want — that would defeat any security use and is unnecessary for tables. Time-sensitive ("random") hashes would break the table entirely.

MCQ 34. (Chapter 3 — Open Addressing) A weakness of Open Addressing (probing) compared with Chaining is that…

a) It cannot store duplicate keys. b) Performance degrades sharply when the load factor exceeds roughly 0.7. ✓ c) It requires the data to be sorted. d) It cannot grow beyond the initial table size.

Correct answer: b) — As the table fills, probe sequences get longer; near 100% full, lookups can approach O(n). Chaining tolerates load factors above 1.0 with graceful degradation. Open Addressing's advantages (better CPU cache locality, no pointer chasing) are offset by the need to keep the table under ~70% full and to use tombstones for deleted entries.

MCQ 35. (Chapter 3 — Dependency graphs) A monitoring dashboard wants to answer "if the database fails, what other services break?". The right algorithmic primitive is…

a) Binary Search across services. b) Bloom filter membership test. c) A DFS-based transitive-closure walk on the dependency graph. ✓ d) A simple sort of services by name.

Correct answer: c) — Dependencies form a directed graph; "everything reachable from X" is the transitive closure of X, computed naturally by DFS or BFS. Production tools like HashiCorp Terraform and AWS CloudFormation rely on graph traversals for the same question in the context of resource dependencies.

MCQ 36. (Chapter 4 — Dijkstra) Dijkstra's algorithm assumes which of the following about edge weights?

a) Edges may have any real-valued weight. b) All edge weights are non-negative. ✓ c) All edge weights are integers. d) All edges have weight 1.

Correct answer: b) — Dijkstra's correctness proof depends on the fact that once a vertex's distance is finalised, no shorter path can later appear through a negative edge. Negative weights invalidate this invariant. For graphs with negative weights, use Bellman-Ford instead — it is slower (O(V × E)) but correct.

MCQ 37. (Chapter 4 — Floyd-Warshall) Floyd-Warshall's time complexity is:

a) O(V + E) b) O((V + E) log V) c) O(V³) ✓ d) O(V × E²)

Correct answer: c) O(V³) — Three nested loops over V, V, V. Floyd-Warshall is the all-pairs shortest-path algorithm based on Dynamic Programming. Acceptable for ~hundreds of vertices, painful for thousands, impossible for millions.

MCQ 38. (Chapter 4 — Routing protocols) Which routing protocol uses Dijkstra's algorithm internally?

a) RIP (Routing Information Protocol). b) OSPF (Open Shortest Path First). ✓ c) BGP (Border Gateway Protocol). d) DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol).

Correct answer: b) — OSPF is a link-state protocol; each router builds a complete map of the network and runs Dijkstra locally. RIP uses Bellman-Ford (distance-vector). BGP is policy-driven and not strictly a shortest-path protocol. DHCP is not a routing protocol at all — it assigns IP (Internet Protocol) addresses. OSPF is standardised by the IETF in RFC 2328.

MCQ 39. (Chapter 4 — BGP) BGP (Border Gateway Protocol) is unlike Dijkstra-style protocols because…

a) It is a centralised protocol controlled by ICANN (Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers). b) It only operates inside a single organisation. c) It is policy-driven, not strictly geometric shortest-path. ✓ d) It is the only protocol that allows negative edge weights.

Correct answer: c) — BGP selects routes based on commercial agreements, peering relationships, and AS-path lengths — not a strict shortest-path metric. Two organisations may prefer a longer geographic route because it crosses cheaper transit networks. BGP is standardised by the IETF in RFC 4271.

MCQ 40. (Chapter 4 — Intractability) A problem is informally called intractable when…

a) It cannot be expressed in code. b) The best known algorithms run in exponential or worse time on its size. ✓ c) It has no known solution at all. d) Its inputs cannot be stored in any data structure.

Correct answer: b) — Intractable problems do have algorithms — just not practical ones. Their best known algorithms grow as O(2ⁿ), O(n!), or worse. Inputs of 50 items can be hopeless. The opposite of intractable is tractable — solvable in polynomial time.

MCQ 41. (Chapter 4 — NP-Completeness) NP-Complete problems share which crucial property?

a) Their inputs can always be cached in memory. b) If any one of them admits a polynomial-time algorithm, all of them do. ✓ c) They each require quantum computers to solve. d) They are all decidable in linear time.

Correct answer: b) — This is the heart of Cook-Levin theorem (1971). NP-complete problems can be reduced to each other in polynomial time, so a fast algorithm for one would imply a fast algorithm for all. Whether such an algorithm exists is the P versus NP question, for which the Clay Mathematics Institute offers a one-million-US-dollar prize.

MCQ 42. (Chapter 4 — TSP) For 100 cities, brute-force enumeration of TSP (Travelling Salesman Problem) tours is infeasible because the number of tours grows as…

a) O(n²) — too slow but doable. b) O(n log n) — manageable on a cluster. c) O(n!) — billions of years even on top hardware. ✓ d) O(2ⁿ) — feasible up to about n = 50.

Correct answer: c) — There are (n−1)!/2 distinct tours fixing the start city; for n = 100 this is roughly 4.7 × 10¹⁵⁵, a 156-digit number. No amount of hardware reaches the heat death of the universe. Even O(2ⁿ) DP-based TSP (the Held-Karp algorithm) is impractical at n = 100, but it pushes the wall to about n = 25.

MCQ 43. (Chapter 4 — Approximation vs heuristic) An approximation algorithm differs from a heuristic because…

a) Heuristics are always written in C; approximations are always written in Python. b) Approximation algorithms come with a provable bound on how far from optimal their answer can be; heuristics do not. ✓ c) Heuristics always run in polynomial time; approximations do not. d) There is no real difference; the terms are synonymous.

Correct answer: b) — An approximation gives a ratio guarantee (e.g., "within 1.5× of optimum"). A heuristic gives no formal guarantee, even if it works very well in practice. The Christofides algorithm is an approximation for TSP; Nearest Neighbour is a heuristic.

MCQ 44. (Chapter 4 — Nearest Neighbour) The Nearest-Neighbour heuristic for TSP produces tours that, on random instances, are on average about…

a) 1% worse than optimal. b) 5% worse than optimal. c) 25% worse than optimal. ✓ d) 200% worse than optimal.

Correct answer: c) — On random TSP instances, Nearest Neighbour empirically produces tours about 25% longer than the optimum on average. On adversarial inputs the ratio can be arbitrarily bad — there is no formal guarantee. For better bounds use Christofides (≤ 1.5×, metric TSP) or Lin-Kernighan with refinements.

MCQ 45. (Chapter 4 — Christofides) The Christofides algorithm for metric TSP (1976) guarantees a tour at most…

a) 1× optimal — it solves TSP exactly. b) 1.5× optimal. ✓ c) 2× optimal. d) 25× optimal.

Correct answer: b) — Christofides combines a minimum spanning tree, a minimum-weight perfect matching of odd-degree vertices, and an Eulerian-tour shortcut to guarantee a tour at most 1.5× the optimum for metric TSP (edge weights satisfy the triangle inequality). It ran in polynomial time and held the best-known approximation factor for ~45 years.

MSQ 1. (Chapter 1 — Sequential Search) Which of the following statements about Sequential Search are true?

Correct answers: a, b, e. Binary Search needs O(1) middle-element access (so linked lists are unsuitable without modification), a sorted order, and a defined comparison. MSQ 3. (Chapter 1 — Greedy) Which of the following classical algorithms are greedy?

Correct answers: a, c, d, f. Each of these commits to the locally optimal pick at every step (smallest tentative distance for Dijkstra, cheapest non-cycle edge for Kruskal, cheapest fringe edge for Prim, two-smallest-frequency merge for Huffman) and never revisits. Correct answers: a, c, d. The 0/1 Knapsack, TSP, and longest simple path require more sophisticated techniques (DP or approximation) and admit easy counter-examples against greedy strategies.

Correct answers: b, d. Greedy choice = the locally best pick is always part of some global optimum. Optimal substructure = optimal answer contains optimal sub-answers. Overlapping subproblems is the DP requirement, not the greedy one. The other options are red herrings.

MSQ 9. (Chapter 2 — DP fundamentals) Which of the following are essential properties for Dynamic Programming to apply?

Correct answers: b, c. Correct answers: a, b, c, e. These four statements are the textbook contrasts. Correct answers: a, c, e, f. MSQ 15. (Chapter 2 — DP design choices) When choosing between memoization and tabulation, which factors favour tabulation?

Correct answers: a, b, d, f. Tabulation wins when every sub-problem will be needed (eager evaluation is "free"), when recursion depth is a concern, when iteration order is straightforward, and when array-based memory access dominates the runtime. MSQ 17. (Chapter 3 — Bloom filters) Which of the following are correct descriptions of Bloom filters?

MSQ 19. (Chapter 3 — Graph representations) Which statements about graph representations are correct?

MSQ 20. (Chapter 3 — BFS uses) Which of the following are appropriate uses of BFS (Breadth-First Search)?

Question. Compare Sequential Search and Binary Search. Give one realistic scenario where Sequential Search is the correct engineering choice even when the data is large, and one scenario where Binary Search is the obviously correct choice. Justify both decisions.

Model answer. Sequential Search walks the collection element by element, comparing each entry against the target. It runs in O(n) and makes no assumption about the data. Binary Search demands that the data already be sorted, then repeatedly halves the search interval, achieving O(log n). The catch is that Binary Search's prerequisite — sorting — costs O(n log n), so a one-off lookup over unsorted data is almost always faster with Sequential Search than with "sort then binary search."

A realistic case for Sequential Search: scanning through an unsorted, append-only log file (for example a Nginx access log) looking for the first occurrence of a particular client IP. The data is large but you only look once, the data is not sorted, and sorting it just to enable Binary Search would cost more than the linear scan itself.

A realistic case for Binary Search: looking up a customer record by primary key inside a sorted index on disk, such as a PostgreSQL B-tree page or an in-memory sorted vector inside a routing table. The data is sorted once and queried millions of times; the log-time lookup compounds into massive savings.

Grader's checklist. Mentions the sorted-data precondition; addresses the "sort then search" amortisation argument; gives concrete and realistic examples, not abstract ones.

Question. Define the greedy-choice property and the optimal substructure property. Use the Activity Selection problem to illustrate both. Why does Activity Selection succeed with a greedy strategy while many superficially similar problems do not?

Model answer. The greedy-choice property says that a globally optimal solution can be built by always taking the locally optimal choice at the current step, without reconsidering past choices. The optimal substructure property says that an optimal solution to a problem contains within it optimal solutions to its subproblems. Greedy algorithms work when both properties hold.

In Activity Selection (also called the Classroom Scheduling problem), you are given a set of activities each with a start and end time, and you must select the largest possible subset of mutually compatible activities. The greedy rule "always pick the activity that finishes earliest among those still compatible" is provably optimal. The greedy-choice property holds because choosing the earliest-finishing activity always leaves the most room for the rest. The optimal substructure property holds because once you commit to that first activity, the remaining problem is a smaller Activity Selection on the still-compatible activities — same shape, smaller input.

Activity Selection succeeds where many problems fail because there is no conflict between local and global optimality: the early-finish rule never "paints you into a corner." Coin Change with the set {1, 3, 4} for amount 6 is a textbook counterexample where greedy fails — taking the largest coin first (4) leaves 2, which costs two more coins for a total of three, while the true optimum is 3+3 for a total of two coins.

Grader's checklist. Defines both properties precisely; uses Activity Selection correctly; explicitly identifies the greedy rule (earliest finish time); contrasts with a problem where greedy fails.

Question. Show, with a small worked example, why the greedy strategy fails on the Coin Change problem with the denomination set {1, 3, 4}. Explain what algorithmic technique replaces greedy here and why it succeeds.

Model answer. Consider making change for the amount 6 with the coin set {1, 3, 4}. Greedy says: take the largest coin that fits — that is 4. Remaining amount is 2. Now the largest coin that fits is 1; take it. Remaining is 1; take another 1. Total: three coins (4, 1, 1). But the true optimum is two coins: (3, 3). Greedy fails because the early commitment to coin 4 blocked the strictly better pair of 3s. The greedy-choice property does not hold here: the local optimum was a global trap.

The technique that replaces greedy is Dynamic Programming (DP). DP succeeds because it does not commit to a single locally-good choice; instead it builds up answers to all sub-amounts from 0 up to the target, and for each sub-amount it considers every coin denomination as a candidate "last coin used." The recurrence is dp[v] = 1 + min(dp[v - c]) for every coin c ≤ v. For amount 6 with coins {1, 3, 4}, DP computes dp[6] = 1 + min(dp[5], dp[3], dp[2]) = 1 + min(2, 1, 2) = 2, giving the correct answer. DP works because it has access to the optimal solutions of all smaller subproblems before deciding the current one — it is greedy without the tunnel vision.

Grader's checklist. Performs the greedy trace correctly (3 coins); states the correct optimum (2 coins); names DP as the replacement; writes the recurrence at least in words; explains why DP avoids the trap (full subproblem knowledge before commitment).

Question. Joshua Bloch, then a Google engineer, famously identified an integer-overflow bug that had been hiding in Binary Search implementations for decades, including in the Java standard library and Sun's JDK. Explain the bug, state the one-line fix, and reflect on what this episode teaches the working programmer.

Model answer. The naïve Binary Search midpoint computation is mid = (low + high) / 2. The bug appears when low + high exceeds the maximum value of the integer type used to hold the sum. In 32-bit signed arithmetic the maximum value is roughly 2.1 billion; the moment low + high crosses that boundary, the sum silently overflows and becomes negative, which makes mid negative, which makes the subsequent array access either crash, return wrong results, or — worst of all — return seemingly correct results that are subtly wrong. The bug is harmless on small arrays and lethal on big ones.

The fix is the one-line replacement mid = low + (high - low) / 2. Mathematically it is equivalent, but high - low is always non-negative and always smaller than high, so the intermediate value never overflows. Joshua Bloch wrote about this in his 2006 Google Research blog post "Nearly All Binary Searches and Mergesorts Are Broken."

The lesson is that a textbook-correct algorithm can be wrong in practice for reasons that have nothing to do with its logic — finite-precision arithmetic, integer wrap-around, hardware limits. Working programmers must understand the gap between mathematics and machines. Code reviews, fuzzing, and property-based testing exist to catch exactly this class of bug.

Grader's checklist. Identifies overflow as the cause; gives the safe fix; correctly attributes to Joshua Bloch; cites the lesson about mathematical correctness vs implementation correctness.

Question. Name four well-known classical algorithms that are greedy in nature: Dijkstra, Kruskal, Prim, and Huffman coding. For each, state the greedy criterion it uses and the problem it solves.

Model answer. Dijkstra's algorithm solves the Single-Source Shortest Path (SSSP) problem on a graph with non-negative edge weights. Its greedy criterion is: at every step, expand the unvisited vertex with the smallest tentative distance from the source. Used inside Google Maps, Waze, and core routing software.

Kruskal's algorithm builds a Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) on a weighted undirected graph. Its greedy criterion is: process all edges in ascending order of weight and add an edge to the MST if and only if it does not create a cycle. Used historically by electric utilities and network architects for least-cost cable layouts.

Prim's algorithm also builds an MST but starts from a single vertex and grows the tree by repeatedly attaching the cheapest edge that connects a tree-vertex to a non-tree-vertex. Same problem as Kruskal, different greedy lens (vertex-centric instead of edge-centric).

Huffman coding builds an optimal prefix-free binary code for a given symbol-frequency distribution, used inside compression formats like GZIP and DEFLATE. Its greedy criterion is: repeatedly merge the two lowest-frequency symbols (or subtrees) into a new combined node and reinsert into a priority queue, until one tree remains.

All four are provably optimal because their respective problems satisfy both the greedy-choice property and the optimal substructure property.

Grader's checklist. Correct problem for each algorithm; correct greedy rule for each; at least one real-world deployment example; recognition that all four are provably optimal.

Question. Dynamic Programming is implemented in one of two styles: top-down with memoization, or bottom-up with tabulation. Compare the two. When would an engineer choose one over the other?

Model answer. Top-down memoization is essentially recursion plus a cache. You write the algorithm in its natural recursive form, but before computing a subproblem you check whether the answer is already in a lookup table; if it is, return it; if not, compute it, store it, and return it. The control flow is the natural recursive call tree, but each unique subproblem is computed exactly once.

Bottom-up tabulation flips the problem upside down. You identify the smallest subproblems, solve them first, store the answers in an array (the "table"), and then build outwards in a controlled iteration order until you reach the answer for the full input. There is no recursion at all; everything is plain loops over a table.

Choose memoization when the recursive structure of the problem is the clearest way to express the solution, when only a sparse subset of subproblems is actually visited (so you would waste memory filling a full table), or when the dependency order is hard to schedule by hand. Choose tabulation when you need predictable performance (no recursion-stack risk, no function-call overhead), when the language has expensive function calls (e.g. Python), when you might want to reduce memory by keeping only the last row or two of the table (a common space-optimisation trick), or when you plan to vectorise the inner loop using a library like NumPy. Industry production code leans toward tabulation because of memory predictability; competitive-programming and interview code often leans toward memoization because it is faster to write.

Grader's checklist. Defines both styles; gives at least two criteria for picking each; mentions stack-depth or recursion-overhead concerns; notes the sparse-subproblems argument for memoization.

Question. Shortest Path on a weighted graph is solvable in polynomial time (e.g. by Dijkstra or Bellman-Ford). The Longest Simple Path problem on the same graph is NP-hard. Explain why — what structural property of one problem allows DP and prevents it for the other?

Model answer. Dynamic Programming requires optimal substructure: an optimal solution must decompose into optimal solutions of subproblems that themselves can be solved independently. Shortest Path has this property. If the shortest path from A to D goes through B and C, then the A-to-B portion is itself the shortest path from A to B, the B-to-C portion is the shortest B-to-C path, and so on. Subproblems are independent: knowing the shortest path from A to B does not constrain the rest of the graph in any subtle way.

Longest Simple Path breaks this. A path is "simple" if it repeats no vertex. The longest simple path from A to D may go through B and C, but the A-to-B portion of that longest path is not necessarily the longest simple A-to-B path on its own — because the A-to-B subpath might have used up vertices that, if avoided, could have allowed an even longer B-to-D extension. The subproblems are entangled: every subpath's optimal answer depends on which vertices it has already visited globally. This entanglement is precisely what makes the problem NP-hard. DP needs subproblems with small, fixed state. Longest Simple Path's state effectively includes "the entire set of vertices used so far," which is exponential in the graph size. This is the same reason the Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP) is hard: subproblems depend on the set of visited cities, not just the current city.

Grader's checklist. Names optimal substructure as the key property; explains the subpath-independence argument for Shortest Path; explains the subpath-entanglement argument for Longest Simple Path; ideally connects this to TSP.

Question. Outline the DP recurrence for the 0/1 Knapsack problem and describe the order in which the DP table is filled. What is the time and space complexity, and how can you recover the actual chosen items after computing the optimal value?

Model answer. In 0/1 Knapsack you have n items, each with a weight w[i] and value v[i], and a knapsack capacity W. You must pick a subset of items maximising total value under the weight constraint, with each item taken at most once. The DP recurrence is:

dp[i][w] = dp[i-1][w]                                  if w[i] > w
dp[i][w] = max(dp[i-1][w], dp[i-1][w - w[i]] + v[i])   otherwise

dp[i][w] represents the best value achievable using only the first i items and capacity w. The table is filled row by row (each row is one item index, from 0 to n) and within each row column by column (from 0 to W). The answer to the full problem is dp[n][W]. Time complexity is O(n·W); space complexity is the same, though it can be reduced to O(W) by keeping only the previous row.

To recover the chosen items, you walk the table backwards from dp[n][W]. At each cell, if dp[i][w] == dp[i-1][w] then item i was not taken — move to dp[i-1][w]. Otherwise item i was taken — record it and move to dp[i-1][w - w[i]]. Continue until you reach row 0. This walk takes O(n) time.

One important caveat: O(n·W) is pseudo-polynomial, not truly polynomial, because W is a numerical value, not the input size. For very large W the DP becomes impractical and approximation algorithms (the FPTAS for Knapsack) take over.

Grader's checklist. Correct recurrence; correct fill order; correct complexity (and ideally mentions O(W) space optimisation); correct backtracking procedure; bonus credit for noting the pseudo-polynomial nature.

Question. Explain how the Bellman-Ford algorithm embodies Dynamic Programming. Where is Bellman-Ford deployed in real networking practice, and why is it preferred over Dijkstra in that setting?

Model answer. Bellman-Ford solves the single-source shortest path problem on a graph that may include negative edge weights, as long as there is no negative-weight cycle reachable from the source. The DP shape of the algorithm is visible in its recurrence: let d[k][v] be the shortest distance from the source to vertex v using at most k edges. Then

d[k][v] = min( d[k-1][v],  min over neighbours u of (d[k-1][u] + weight(u, v)) )

The algorithm runs |V| - 1 rounds; in each round it "relaxes" every edge, propagating shorter paths one extra hop deep. After |V| - 1 rounds, all shortest paths of any length are guaranteed found, because no simple path can have more than |V| - 1 edges. The optimal-substructure property is exploited classically: a shortest path of length k is the shortest path of length k-1 to some predecessor plus one final edge.

Bellman-Ford is the algorithmic core of RIP (Routing Information Protocol), standardised in RFC 2453. RIP is a distance-vector protocol: each router stores a vector of distances to every known destination and periodically exchanges this vector with its neighbours. The relaxation step in Bellman-Ford corresponds exactly to "I just heard from neighbour X that it can reach destination D in 5 hops; my current distance to D is 8; therefore my new distance via X is 6." Bellman-Ford is preferred over Dijkstra here for two reasons: (1) it is naturally distributed — no router needs the global graph, only neighbour summaries; (2) it tolerates the kinds of edge metrics RIP uses (and in extended variants, even negative ones), where Dijkstra would silently produce wrong results.

The trade-off is convergence speed and the "count-to-infinity" problem, which is why modern large networks have largely migrated to link-state protocols like OSPF (Dijkstra-based). Bellman-Ford remains important pedagogically and as the substrate for understanding distance-vector routing.

Grader's checklist. States the recurrence or its idea; correctly explains the |V|-1 round count; identifies RIP as the routing application; explains the distributed/local-knowledge argument for why RIP uses Bellman-Ford; bonus credit for mentioning count-to-infinity.

Question. Describe how Quality of Service (QoS) routing generalises the basic shortest-path DP to handle multiple constraints simultaneously. Give a concrete example.

Model answer. Basic shortest-path DP optimises a single scalar — usually total weight along the path. QoS routing asks for a path that simultaneously satisfies multiple objectives or constraints: minimise latency and maximise bandwidth and keep packet-loss probability below a threshold and stay within a cost budget. The DP recurrence generalises by carrying a richer state per subproblem. Instead of dp[v] = minimum cost to reach v, the state becomes dp[v][b][l] = some property of the best path to v using bandwidth class b and within latency budget l — or, more practically, a Pareto-front of non-dominated paths to each vertex.

A concrete example: an Iberia Cargo flight planner needs to route a perishable shipment from Madrid-Barajas to Toronto-Pearson. The objectives are (1) minimise total transit time, (2) keep cabin-temperature exposure under a threshold, (3) avoid airports with handling delays above a limit, and (4) stay within fuel budget. A simple Dijkstra cannot express all four. A multi-constraint DP computes, for each candidate handoff airport, the Pareto-front of routes reaching it — meaning the set of routes where no single route is better on every dimension. The final selection picks from the Pareto-front based on the customer's priority ordering.

This kind of multi-constraint shortest-path is NP-hard in the general case, which is why production systems use heuristics, ILP solvers like Gurobi or open-source CBC, or relaxations that drop some constraints to soft penalties.

Grader's checklist. Defines QoS routing as multi-objective; explains the state-explosion argument; gives a concrete and plausible example; ideally notes the NP-hardness and the practical workaround using solvers or heuristics.

Question. Hash tables resolve collisions with one of two main strategies: separate chaining and open addressing. Compare them in terms of memory usage, performance under high load, deletion semantics, and the kind of workload that suits each.

Model answer. Separate chaining stores each hash-bucket as a list (or a small tree). When two keys hash to the same bucket, both live in the bucket's list. Insertion is straightforward — append to the list. Lookup hashes to a bucket and walks the list. Deletion is also straightforward — remove from the list. Memory usage includes the bucket array plus per-key list-node overhead (pointer/next-pointer per entry), which can be significant for many small values.

Open addressing stores all entries directly in the bucket array itself. When a collision occurs, the algorithm probes other slots (linearly, quadratically, or via double-hashing) until it finds an empty one. Lookup probes in the same sequence until it finds the key or an empty slot. Memory usage is leaner — no list-node overhead — but the table must always have a meaningful number of empty slots to function, so it cannot be filled past a "load factor" of roughly 0.7–0.8.

Performance under high load. Chaining degrades more gracefully — even at a load factor of 1.5, lookups still work, just slower. Open addressing degrades sharply as the load factor approaches 1; primary clustering ruins linear probing in particular.

Deletion semantics. Chaining handles deletion trivially. Open addressing must use tombstone markers — deleted slots can't simply be set to empty without breaking the probe sequence of other keys. Tombstones complicate the code and require periodic table rebuilds.

Workload fit. Chaining is preferred for general-purpose hash tables, especially those with frequent deletions or unpredictable load. Open addressing is preferred when memory locality matters (the entries are physically packed, which is friendlier to the CPU cache) and when deletions are rare. Many production-grade implementations like the Go map and Python dict use sophisticated open-addressing variants because of cache-locality benefits.

Grader's checklist. Defines both strategies; compares memory; addresses load-factor degradation; raises tombstones explicitly for open-addressing deletion; gives at least one production example.

Question. Explain how a Bloom filter works and why its false-positive behaviour is acceptable in real systems. Give two concrete deployments.

Model answer. A Bloom filter is a probabilistic data structure that answers set-membership queries — "is x in the set?" — using far less memory than a conventional hash table. It consists of a bit array of m bits, initially all zero, and k independent hash functions. Insertion of a key x computes the k hash values, takes each modulo m, and sets the corresponding bits to 1. Query of a key x computes the same k bits and asks whether they are all 1. If any of them is 0, the answer is a definite NO — the key is not in the set. If all are 1, the answer is PROBABLY YES — the key may be in the set, or those bits may have been set collectively by other keys (a false positive).

The trade-off is asymmetric and that asymmetry is the whole point. False negatives are impossible: a Bloom filter never lies and says "no" when the answer is "yes." False positives are possible but their rate can be tuned by sizing m and k appropriately. The pay-off is that a Bloom filter uses roughly 10 bits per element for a 1% false-positive rate, regardless of how big each element is — strings, URLs, hashes, anything.

Concrete deployments. (1) Bitcoin's SPV (Simplified Payment Verification) clients use Bloom filters to ask full nodes "do any transactions in this block involve any of my addresses?" without revealing exactly which addresses belong to the client — described in BIP 37. The false-positive rate gives the client meaningful privacy. (2) Chrome's Safe Browsing protocol stores known-malicious URLs in a local Bloom filter; if a URL hits the filter, Chrome then asks Google's server for confirmation. Most URLs miss the filter and require no network call, saving bandwidth and protecting privacy. False positives just cause an extra round-trip to the server. (3) Cassandra uses per-SSTable Bloom filters so that a read query can quickly skip SSTables that definitely do not contain the requested key, avoiding expensive disk reads.

Grader's checklist. Defines insertion and query operations correctly; identifies false-positive direction (never false negative); explains why the asymmetry is useful; gives at least two real deployments.

Question. Plain hash(key) mod N works for a fixed N, but breaks badly when servers are added or removed from a distributed cache or storage cluster. Explain how consistent hashing solves this problem and quantify the savings.

Model answer. Imagine a cluster of N cache servers using the rule server = hash(key) mod N. If you add one more server (N → N+1), the modulus changes, and approximately N / (N+1) of all keys now map to a different server. For a 100-server cluster adding one new server, that's about 99% of keys becoming "wrong" — they would all have to be re-fetched from the origin database, causing a thundering-herd traffic spike that can take the origin down.

Consistent hashing fixes this by hashing keys and servers onto the same conceptual ring (typically a 32-bit or 128-bit hash space arranged as a circle). A key is assigned to the first server encountered when walking the ring clockwise from the key's position. Adding a new server only steals the keys in the arc immediately preceding the new server's position on the ring; removing a server only redistributes its arc to the next server clockwise. On average only 1/N of keys move on a single server change, instead of nearly all of them.

A refinement called virtual nodes — each physical server is hashed to many ring positions instead of one — smooths the load distribution so no single server ends up with a disproportionately large arc.

Consistent hashing was introduced in a 1997 paper by David Karger and colleagues at MIT and is the foundation of Akamai's CDN, Amazon's Dynamo paper (which shaped DynamoDB, Cassandra, and Riak), and the routing logic of Memcached client libraries. The savings are dramatic: a single server addition or failure causes ~1/N of cache misses, not ~99%.

Grader's checklist. Explains the hash mod N failure mode quantitatively; describes the ring structure; identifies the 1/N savings; mentions virtual nodes; gives at least one production example.

Question. Compare the adjacency list and adjacency matrix representations of a graph. State the memory cost, the cost of checking whether edge (u, v) exists, and the cost of iterating over all neighbours of a vertex. Which representation suits sparse graphs and which suits dense graphs?

Model answer. Let V be the number of vertices and E the number of edges.

Adjacency matrix. A V × V 2-D array, where entry (u, v) holds the edge weight (or 0/null if no edge). Memory: O(V²) regardless of how many edges actually exist. Edge existence check (u, v): O(1) — direct table lookup. Iterate over neighbours of u: O(V) — must scan the entire row, including non-edges.

Adjacency list. An array (or hash map) indexed by vertex, where each entry holds a list of (neighbour, weight) pairs for the outgoing edges of that vertex. Memory: O(V + E). Edge existence check (u, v): O(degree(u)) — must walk u's list looking for v, unless the list is augmented with a set. Iterate over neighbours of u: O(degree(u)) — exactly the neighbours, nothing else.

Suitability. Adjacency matrix wins on dense graphs (E close to V²), where the matrix is nearly full anyway, where edge-existence checks dominate the workload, and where matrix-level operations (e.g. transitive closure via Floyd-Warshall or matrix-multiplication algorithms) are natural. Adjacency list wins on sparse graphs (E much smaller than V²), which is the overwhelmingly common case in real networks: a road map of Spain has millions of intersections but each intersection has only a handful of roads, so the matrix would be 99.99% empty. Social graphs, road graphs, the Internet's BGP graph, and most production graph workloads use adjacency lists.

Grader's checklist. Correct memory bounds for both; correct lookup and iteration costs; correctly identifies which is sparse-friendly and which is dense-friendly; ideally gives a concrete real-world example for each preference.

Question. Compare Breadth-First Search (BFS) and Depth-First Search (DFS) in terms of the data structure each uses, their time and space complexity, and the kinds of problems each is suited to.

Model answer. BFS explores a graph in layers, visiting all vertices at distance 1 from the source before any vertex at distance 2, and so on. It uses a queue (FIFO — First In, First Out). Time complexity is O(V + E) for an adjacency-list graph. Space complexity is O(V) for the visited set plus the queue, which in the worst case can hold one full layer of the graph.

DFS explores a graph by plunging as deep as possible along one path before backtracking. It uses a stack (LIFO — Last In, First Out), either explicitly or implicitly through recursion. Time complexity is also O(V + E). Space complexity is O(V) for visited tracking plus the recursion stack, which can equal the longest path in the graph (a problem on pathological inputs).

Problem fit. BFS is the right tool when you need the shortest path in an unweighted graph (number of hops), when exploring layer by layer matters (e.g. friend-of-friend recommendation in a social network up to depth 3), when a web crawler needs to visit pages closest to the seed first, or when computing a spanning tree level by level. DFS is the right tool for cycle detection, topological sorting of a DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) for build systems and task schedulers, finding strongly-connected components, generating tree traversals (pre-order, in-order, post-order), and constraint-satisfaction / backtracking searches (Sudoku solvers, maze solvers, puzzle solvers).

A useful rule of thumb: if "level" or "shortest unweighted distance" appears in the problem statement, reach for BFS. If "order of dependencies," "cycle," "all paths," or "backtracking" appears, reach for DFS.

Grader's checklist. Queue/FIFO for BFS, Stack/LIFO for DFS; correct complexities; at least two correct problem domains for each; the heuristic about shortest-path vs ordering is bonus credit.

Question. Dijkstra's algorithm is correct for shortest paths with non-negative edge weights only. Explain why it fails on negative weights, and describe how Bellman-Ford correctly handles them.

Model answer. Dijkstra works on a greedy commitment: once a vertex is dequeued from the priority queue with its tentative distance, that distance is treated as final — Dijkstra never reconsiders it. This commitment is sound when all edges are non-negative, because any further path discovered later through other vertices can only add to the distance, never reduce it. Negative edges break that invariant. Suppose we dequeue vertex B with distance 5 from source A, and later we discover a path A → C → B where the C→B edge has weight −7. The true shortest A-to-B distance is now (whatever A→C costs) − 7, which could be less than 5. But Dijkstra has already moved on from B and will not revisit it, so it returns the wrong answer.

Bellman-Ford does not make any final commitments. Instead, it relaxes every edge |V| - 1 times, in successive rounds. This guarantees that after round k, every shortest-path of at most k edges has been correctly computed. After |V| - 1 rounds, all simple paths have had a chance to be discovered. Negative edges pose no problem because relaxation is symmetric — it accepts shorter paths regardless of how they arose. Bellman-Ford additionally detects negative-weight cycles: if any edge can still be relaxed in a hypothetical |V|-th round, a negative cycle is reachable from the source, and shortest paths to vertices in that cycle are undefined (you can loop the cycle to drive distance to −∞).

The trade-off is performance: Bellman-Ford runs in O(V·E), while Dijkstra with a binary heap runs in O((V + E) log V). On modern networks with potentially millions of edges, Dijkstra is dramatically faster, which is why most Internet routing protocols choose link-state designs over distance-vector ones.

Grader's checklist. Identifies the greedy-finalisation argument as Dijkstra's weakness; correctly describes Bellman-Ford's relaxation rounds; mentions negative-cycle detection; states the complexity trade-off.

Question. Internet routing protocols fall into two main families: Distance-Vector and Link-State. Compare them, name at least one real protocol in each family, and explain why most large modern networks have moved to Link-State.

Model answer. Distance-Vector protocols treat each router as a local agent that only knows the distances reported by its immediate neighbours. Each router maintains a vector of (destination, distance, next-hop) entries and periodically shares this vector with its neighbours. The neighbours update their own vectors using a Bellman-Ford-style relaxation. The router never sees the global topology. Real protocols: RIP (Routing Information Protocol), standardised in RFC 2453, and EIGRP (Enhanced Interior Gateway Routing Protocol), a Cisco protocol that was later opened.

Link-State protocols take the opposite approach. Every router floods a description of its directly attached links to every other router in the network. Once each router has the complete link-state database, it independently runs Dijkstra's algorithm on its local copy of the global graph to compute shortest paths. Real protocols: OSPF (Open Shortest Path First), standardised in RFC 2328, and IS-IS (Intermediate System to Intermediate System), originally an ISO/OSI protocol now widely used inside ISP (Internet Service Provider) backbones.

Why Link-State dominates large networks. Distance-Vector protocols suffer from the count-to-infinity problem: when a link fails, bad routing information propagates slowly hop by hop, and routers can spend many rounds (and many lost packets) converging to the new state — sometimes oscillating in the meantime. Mitigations like split-horizon and route-poisoning help but do not eliminate the problem. Link-State protocols, in contrast, flood topology changes globally and immediately, and every router recomputes shortest paths independently. Convergence is faster, deterministic, and easier to reason about — at the cost of more CPU per router and more memory to hold the full database. For a small home network RIP is fine; for Telefónica or Bell Canada's backbone you want OSPF or IS-IS.

Grader's checklist. Defines both families correctly; names at least one protocol per family with a working hyperlink; explains count-to-infinity as the distance-vector weakness; explains the CPU/memory trade-off of link-state.

Question. Explain what BGP (Border Gateway Protocol) is, why it is fundamentally different from intra-domain protocols like OSPF, and discuss what happened during the October 2021 Facebook outage at the BGP level.

Model answer. BGP is the protocol that glues the Internet's many independently operated networks — called Autonomous Systems (AS) — together. Inside one organisation (say Bell Canada or Telefónica), routers use an interior routing protocol like OSPF or IS-IS to find shortest paths. But the moment a packet has to leave one organisation and enter another, the question stops being "which path is shortest?" and becomes "which path is allowed and preferred under our commercial and policy agreements?" That is BGP's job: it advertises reachability of IP prefixes between AS, lets each AS apply commercial filters (peering relationships, transit contracts, customer routes), and ultimately decides which next-AS to hand a packet to. BGP is standardised in RFC 4271.

The crucial difference from OSPF: BGP is policy-driven, not shortest-path. The path a packet takes between Madrid and Toronto is determined by which transit providers Telefónica and Bell Canada have peering or transit agreements with, who is cheaper, and any prefix-level policy rules — not by physical distance. This is why packets sometimes take seemingly absurd detours.

On 4 October 2021, Facebook (now Meta) experienced a global outage lasting roughly six hours. The root cause was a BGP misconfiguration: a routine maintenance command, intended to assess backbone capacity, was issued in a way that withdrew the BGP advertisements for Facebook's authoritative DNS servers. Once those advertisements were withdrawn, the rest of the Internet had no route to Facebook's DNS — and without DNS, no user could resolve facebook.com, instagram.com, or whatsapp.com. Worse, Facebook's internal tools, badge-readers, and remote-access systems also relied on the same DNS, so engineers could not log in remotely to fix the problem and had to physically enter the data centres. The incident is a textbook illustration of two ideas: (1) BGP is a control plane that, when misconfigured, can erase you from the Internet; (2) operational dependencies should not be circular — never put the system you rely on to fix outages inside the system that just broke.

Grader's checklist. Correctly defines BGP and AS; explains the policy-driven nature; correctly attributes the Facebook outage to BGP withdrawal; mentions the DNS-dependency angle; ideally draws an operational/architectural lesson.

Question. State the P vs NP question in plain language. Explain what NP-completeness means and what role the Cook-Levin theorem plays in the theory.

Model answer. P is the class of decision problems for which a solution can be computed in polynomial time — a polynomial in the size of the input. NP is the class of decision problems for which a proposed solution can be verified in polynomial time. Trivially every problem in P is in NP (because you can verify by recomputing). The famous question P vs NP asks: is every NP problem also in P? Or, in plainer language: if a solution can be quickly checked, can it also be quickly found?

Nobody knows the answer. Most researchers suspect P ≠ NP — that some problems are genuinely harder to solve than to verify — but no proof exists in either direction. The Clay Mathematics Institute lists P vs NP among its seven Millennium Prize Problems with a one-million-dollar prize for a proof.

NP-complete problems are the hardest problems in NP, in a precise sense: every problem in NP can be polynomial-time reduced to any NP-complete problem. If anyone ever finds a polynomial-time algorithm for any single NP-complete problem, then P = NP and every problem in NP collapses to P at once. Conversely, proving any NP-complete problem requires super-polynomial time would prove P ≠ NP.

The Cook-Levin theorem, proved independently by Stephen Cook in 1971 and Leonid Levin (also in 1971, behind the Iron Curtain), established that the Boolean Satisfiability Problem (SAT) is NP-complete. This was the first NP-completeness result, and it founded the entire theory. Once SAT was known to be NP-complete, Richard Karp's 1972 paper showed 21 further problems were NP-complete by reducing SAT to them, including TSP, the Knapsack decision problem, Hamiltonian Cycle, Vertex Cover, and Graph Colouring. Today thousands of problems are known to be NP-complete, and the Cook-Levin theorem sits at the root of the entire reduction tree.

Grader's checklist. Correctly states P and NP in solution-vs-verification language; defines NP-completeness via reduction; correctly attributes Cook-Levin and names SAT; mentions the Clay Millennium prize; bonus credit for naming Karp's 21 follow-up problems.

Question. Suppose your manager hands you a problem that turns out to be NP-complete — say, finding the optimal delivery route for a fleet of vehicles (a TSP variant). You cannot solve it optimally at scale in polynomial time. What are your three main engineering options, and how would you choose between them? Use TSP (Travelling Salesman Problem) as the running example.

Model answer. When confronted with an NP-complete problem at production scale, an engineer has three main options.

Option 1 — Brute force. If the input is genuinely small (for TSP, ≤ ~12–15 cities for interactive response; up to ~25 with Held-Karp DP), just compute the optimum. There is no shame in this. A pizza-delivery driver visiting 8 customers does not need approximation theory; modern computers can enumerate 8! = 40,320 tours in microseconds. The trap is assuming the input will stay small; engineers should always ask "what happens at 100 cities?" before settling on brute force.

Option 2 — Approximation algorithm with a provable bound. For metric TSP (where the triangle inequality holds), the Christofides–Serdyukov algorithm is guaranteed to produce a tour at most 1.5× the optimum. The guarantee is a mathematical theorem, not an empirical observation, and that matters when you need contractual assurance: a logistics company can promise customers that routes are within X% of optimum and back that promise with proof.

Option 3 — Heuristic with strong empirical behaviour. Algorithms like Lin-Kernighan, simulated annealing, and genetic algorithms offer no worst-case guarantee but routinely produce tours within 1% of optimum on real-world instances of thousands of cities, often in seconds. The implementation LKH by Keld Helsgaun is the world record holder on many benchmark instances. The trade-off is intellectual honesty: you cannot promise the customer a worst-case bound, only empirical results on representative data.

A fourth and increasingly important option is to use a specialised exact solver such as the Concorde TSP Solver, which combines branch-and-bound, cutting planes, and ILP techniques to solve genuine TSP instances with thousands of cities — slowly, but exactly. For one-off planning problems where you have hours and need the true optimum, this is the right answer.

How to choose. Ask three questions. (1) How big is the input? If small, brute force or Held-Karp DP. (2) Do I need a provable bound? If yes, Christofides. (3) Do I need real-time response at scale? If yes, heuristic (Lin-Kernighan, LKH). Companies like Amazon, Glovo, Canada Post, and Bombardier solve TSP-shaped problems daily, almost always with heuristic methods combined with domain-specific pruning, because real fleet routing also has time windows, vehicle capacities, driver shift rules, and dozens of other constraints that exact solvers struggle with at scale.

Grader's checklist. Names all three options explicitly; gives one concrete algorithm or solver per option (Christofides, Lin-Kernighan, LKH or Concorde); explains the trade-offs honestly; gives at least one real-company example.
